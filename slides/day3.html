Why a holdout

Generators see only the training patients. The holdout scores utility (Lab 3.3) and is the "never seen" group every attack compares with (Lab 3.4).

The tables are pseudonymised: study_id, no names. Patient columns: sex, birth year, region, nationality, diabetes, CKD, heart failure, prior admissions, site. Encounter columns: month, length of stay, ward, diagnosis group, number of medicines, creatinine, glucose, discharge, readmitted. The holdout is 30 percent of patients, drawn at random with seed 405.

Synthetic data

A model of the data, sampled: good at patterns, bad at individuals

Good for

  • Developing and testing code before access to real data
  • Teaching and data challenges
  • Patterns the generator was built to keep

Not for

  • Exact counts, especially small ones
  • Rare groups, outliers, a named patient
  • Relationships the generator did not model
And not automatically private. Privacy is a property to measure by attack, not a property of the word.

Source: Stadler, Oprisanu and Troncoso, "Synthetic Data, Anonymisation Groundhog Day" (USENIX Security 2022): synthetic data does not by default give a better privacy-utility trade-off than older anonymisation, and its gain is hard to predict. Recap from DS-309 Day 2 for anyone who took it: one table, DCR and membership inference. Today: two related tables, per-table attacks and a vendor file.

The choice

Three ways to answer a request, each with its own residual risk

OptionThe requester getsResidual risk
Synthetic filerows drawn from a model of the dataleaks if the generator memorises; analysis biased where it loses relations

The pseudonymisation guideline is explicit that pseudonymised data stays personal data and that the extra information must be kept separately. An extract therefore needs a lawful basis, an agreement and controls, exactly as the source table does. A query service is what Day 4's monthly statistics and secure aggregation are versions of.

From Lab 3.2 step 1 on the training tables. The number of children is part of the parent: sicker patients come back more often. A generator for related tables must model it, and must draw the children of each parent conditioned on that parent.

Two generators

A floor, and one that conditions each column on the ones before it

Independent baseline

Every column drawn from its own distribution. Perfect marginals, no relationships. The floor any generator must beat.

Conditional trees

Column by column: a tree fitted on the columns before it, a value copied from a random real row of the same leaf (at least 25 rows). The CART method of synthpop.

Both run on a laptop CPU in seconds. SDV's HMASynthesizer is the stretch, in Colab.

Source for the tree method: Nowok, Raab and Dibben, synthpop (Journal of Statistical Software, 2016), which uses classification and regression trees as its default. SDV's HMASynthesizer is a hierarchical model of the same idea: parent table with child-count and child-parameter columns. It installs a deep-learning stack, which is why it is the Colab stretch and not the core lab. The course writes its own small generator so that every step is visible.

Privacy by attack

Two attacks, both against a group the generator never saw

Distance to closest record

For each synthetic record: is the nearest real record a member (training) or a non-member (holdout)? No copying: about 0.5.

Membership inference

For each real record: score "was in training" by its distance to the nearest synthetic record. AUC 0.5 is guessing, 1.0 is certainty.

Members are a random 1,849 of the training patients, as many as the holdout.

Equal group sizes make 0.5 the no-leak value for both measures. Distances: each column coded and scaled by its spread in the training data, then Euclidean. Patients are compared with patients, encounters with encounters, because a generator can be safe on one table and copy the other.

Checkpoint

Reading an attack

A membership-inference test on a synthetic encounter table gives an AUC of 0.51; on the patient table, 0.88. What do you conclude?

The release is safe on average
Patient attributes are coarse, so the 0.88 does not matter
The encounter test failed and should be re-run
The patient table leaks its training patients; the release fails until it is fixed
Why. A release passes only if every table passes: an attacker uses the weakest one, and the tables link through the identifier.

Answer: D. B is tempting after the exact-twins slide: coarse attributes produce chance matches, but those affect members and non-members equally; 0.88 means members are found far more often.

Walk the room. Usual problems: using all 4,151 training patients as members against 1,849 non-members (0.5 is then no longer the no-leak value); coding text differently in two tables; reading exact matches as the result. The run takes about a minute; nearest neighbours on 9,000 rows are fast on a laptop.
