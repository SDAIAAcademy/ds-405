Session 1

Disclosure risk in longitudinal records

  • Measure uniqueness of encounter sequences as quasi-identifiers
  • Compare longitudinal risk with the single-table estimate
  • Reduce the risk by coarsening and truncation and measure the cost
01
The usual estimate

On one table, 4% of patients are unique

4.2%
of 4,151 patients unique on sex, year of birth and region

Recap from DS-309

Uniqueness and k-anonymity on a single table. Today the table has a history attached: stays, months, diagnoses.

Participants who took DS-309 know this measure. The question of the session: is 4% the right number to report for an extract that includes every patient's stays? The lab answers it on the same 4,151 training patients.

The adversary

A relative knows your stays, not your record

Facts

Sex and year of birth: anyone who knows you.

Stays

"In hospital in March for his heart, again in July": a token of month and diagnosis group per stay.

The test

How many patients match the facts and have every known token? One means found.

The lab takes the first k stays of each patient as what the adversary knows: a simplification, since an adversary may know any k. Matching uses containment: the candidate's stays must include all known tokens, not equal them.

From the course's run of Lab 4.1. The single-table 4.2% under-states the risk of the same extract nearly tenfold once one stay is known (40.1%), and more than twentyfold at two known stays (96.6%). The sequence of stays is a quasi-identifier of its own, and the most identifying column of a longitudinal extract.

Lab 4.1 · step 4

Coarsening helps; truncation made it worse

ReleaseAdversary knowsUnique
as issex, year of birth, 2 stays96.6%
coarsened: decade, quartersex, decade, 2 stays20.6%

Truncation dropped 24% of the stays, the repeat stays where readmissions happen.

The surprise of the lab: truncating to two stays per patient raised uniqueness at two known stays from 21% to 47%. Other patients' later stays were the crowd a person hid in; remove them and fewer candidates contain the known tokens. Measure every lever; do not assume its direction. ARX and sdcMicro implement these transformations with many more options; they are named here, not run, because they are a Java tool and an R package.

Checkpoint

Which number to report

An extract of patients with all their stays shows 4% of patients unique on sex, year of birth and region. What do you tell the release board?

The risk is low: 4%
The 4% ignores the stays: with two known stays nearly every patient with two is unique
Release only the first two stays of each patient and the risk is solved
The study identifiers are pseudonyms, so uniqueness does not matter
Why. An adversary's knowledge of stays makes the sequence a quasi-identifier: 96.6% unique at two known stays.

Answer: B. C is the lab's surprise: truncation raised risk at two known stays to 47%.

Session 2

Privacy budgets over many releases

  • Bound each person's contribution to a monthly statistic
  • Allocate one budget across twelve releases and keep a ledger
  • Plot error against budget and show why re-releases must be charged
02
Recap from DS-309

Noise calibrated to how much one person can change the answer

  • Privacy unit: whose presence is hidden (here: a patient).
  • Contribution bound: the most one unit changes the statistic.
  • Laplace scale = bound ÷ ε; expected absolute error = scale.

New today

Twelve releases, not one. Every release spends budget, and the spends add up: sequential composition.

Sources: Dwork and Roth, The Algorithmic Foundations of Differential Privacy; NIST SP 800-226 on evaluating guarantees, which insists that the unit of privacy and the accounting over all releases are stated. DS-309 did Laplace counts at three budgets and split one budget over four queries; this session adds the year of releases and the ledger.

Lab 4.2 · step 1

Count readmitted patients, not readmitted stays

Stays

One patient had up to 3 readmitted stays in a month: the noise would need to cover 3.

Patients

Each patient moves each month's count by at most 1: about 140 to 200 a month in 2025.

All 6,000 study patients, train and holdout: the dashboard counts everyone. Bounding the contribution is a choice about the statistic, made before any noise. It also changes what is published: patients readmitted, not readmissions. Say that on the dashboard.

Checkpoint

The nightly dashboard

The ministry's dashboard recomputes every past month each night with fresh noise at ε = 1/12. The ledger is charged only once per month. What happens?

Anyone who averages the nightly values recovers the true counts; the real spend grows every night

Why. Every noisy copy is a release. A year of copies brought January within two patients of the truth.

Answer: C. D would be the good outcome: a ledger that is actually charged refuses on night 13.

Session 3

Analysis without pooling records

  • Explain federated analysis, secure aggregation and clean rooms
  • Compute a pooled rate with a simulated secure-aggregation protocol
  • Identify what the pooled output still discloses
03
Three ways

Analysis where the records stay

Federated analysis

The code travels to each site; only results come back.

Secure aggregation

Sites send masked numbers; only their sum can be read.

Clean room

A controlled environment where approved queries run on joined data and outputs are checked.

Each protects the inputs. None protects the output by itself.

Federated learning is one form of federated analysis. Secure aggregation is the protocol of Bonawitz et al. (2017), designed for many devices with dropouts; the lab simulates its two ideas, pairwise masks and dropout recovery, for three hospital groups. Clean rooms are offered by cloud vendors; the course names the idea only.

Lab 4.3 · steps 2 and 3

Masks that cancel in the sum

1 · Agreeeach pair of sites shares a random mask
→
2 · Maskadd masks with later sites, subtract with earlier ones
→
3 · Sumthe aggregator adds three random-looking numbers: the masks cancel
Result. 2,023 readmitted of 13,258 stays: 15.26%, exact, and no site's count was ever seen.

All arithmetic modulo the prime 2^61 − 1, so a masked value is uniformly random. Site counts: Sidra 688 of 4,683, Ufuq 681 of 4,524, Rawdah 654 of 4,051. The additive-shares step does the same with every site sending a share to every other: simpler, and more messages.

Lab 4.3 · step 4

A site drops out after agreeing its masks

  • Two masked values arrive; Rawdah's masks are left in the sum.
  • The survivors reveal the masks they shared with Rawdah; the aggregator removes them: 1,369.

The rule that follows

Once recovery starts, a late value from Rawdah is refused: its masks are now known, so it could be unmasked alone.

In the real protocol each site secret-shares its mask seeds in advance, so the survivors can reconstruct a dropped site's masks without that site. The lab reveals them directly to keep the idea visible.

Lab 4.3 · step 5

The total is exact, so subtraction works

Two sites collude

Total 2,023 minus Sidra's 688 and Ufuq's 681: Rawdah's 654, exactly.

A small subgroup

Obstetric stays followed by readmission: total 17. The other two subtract theirs: Rawdah had 2.

Protect the output too: minimum sites and people per cell, and noise on the published total (the stretch adds Laplace noise, ε = 0.5).

Secure aggregation answers "who sees the inputs during computation". It does not answer "what does the result disclose". The planted problem of the session is this subtraction: a two-site sum reveals the third.

Checkpoint

What the protocol protects

Three hospital groups compute a pooled readmission count by secure aggregation and publish it. Which statement is true?

The aggregator never sees a site's count, but two sites together can compute the third's from the published total
No one can learn anything about any site's count
The published total is approximate because of the masks
The aggregator must be trusted with every site's count
Why. The masks cancel exactly: the output is the true total, and subtraction by those who know the other inputs reveals the last one.

Answer: A. C is common: masks are not noise, they cancel completely.

The planted problem of Day 4: the request lacks the written approval of the applicant's academic authority, which the General Rules for Secondary Use of Data require of an academic applicant, who applies through the institution. Ask the room to find three other problems before the next slide.

The national frameworks

What the request is tested against

Data Sharing Policy: eight principles

Data sharing culture, single source of truth, legitimate purpose, authorized access, transparency, collective accountability, data security, ethical data use.

Secondary use: the applicant

Legitimate purpose, not profit-oriented; the minimum necessary data; to the data source entity; an academic applicant through the institution with written approval; the request form.

And the PDPL: health data is sensitive; pseudonymised data remains personal data.

Read from the published texts: the NDMO Data Sharing Policy, the General Rules for Secondary Use of Data, the PDPL and its implementing regulation, and the guideline on anonymisation and pseudonymisation. The classification policy's levels (Top Secret, Secret, Restricted with sub-levels A to C, Public) give the classification field of the record. The course does not interpret these texts beyond what they say; the decision of what applies to a real request belongs to the entity's legal and data-protection functions.

Checkpoint

The decision

The synthetic pair passes every attack. The university's request still lacks its academic authority's written approval. What does the board decide?

Approve: the file is safe, so the approval is a formality
Reject the request permanently
Conditional approval: nothing moves until the request comes through the institution with written approval, on the form
Send a de-identified extract instead, which needs no approval
Why. An academic applicant applies through the institution with written approval of its academic authority; the method decides what is released once the request is complete.

Day 4 · wrap-up

Three things to keep

Histories identify

Measure risk with what an adversary knows; measure every lever.

Budgets are accounts

Bound each person, charge every release, cache what you publish.

Decide on evidence

Rules first, then method, residual risk, conditions and a named approver.

Point to the capstone: one of three requests, a release package, a ten-minute defence that re-runs one attack on a table the assessor chooses.
